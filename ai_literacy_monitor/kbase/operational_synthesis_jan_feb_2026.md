## I. AIX OER SPRING 2026: FROM PLAN TO LAUNCH
### The Collaborative Structure
The AIX OER project moved from concept to operational reality between December 2025 and January 2026. The core parameters:
- **10 participants** across roles: graduate students, faculty, librarians, instructional designers, international partners
- **$1,500 budget**: 10 participants × $30/month × 5 months for Claude Team Projects
- **Duration**: January–May 2026
- **Participation tiers**: Lurker, Observer, Contributor, Student—allowing variable commitment levels
The invitation email (circulated December 2025) established the project's identity: "This is collaborative work applying a specific definition of literacy—reading, thinking, and writing using the tools, techniques, and technological systems of our peers—to the challenge of teaching with and about AI."
### Three-Platform Architecture in Practice
The platform design reflects distinct functional roles:
**Microsoft Teams** serves as the institutional backbone—repository for markdown conversation archives, coordination space for known collaborators, and the primary working layer for materials under development. SharePoint integration provides institutional file access. This is where the "messy" development work lives.
**Claude Team Projects** functions as shared AI workspace with collective memory. All project files, synthesis documents, and accumulated context live here. Crucially, all participant work within the Claude Team Project is visible to other participants—this transparency is intentional, creating reflexive practice where development work becomes primary source material. The shared project context means every participant's AI conversations benefit from the accumulated knowledge of the collaborative.
**D2L/Brightspace** handles official LMS integration—grades, institutional compliance, the machinery that must live within the learning management system. Used primarily for IDT 590 course delivery.
**Wikiversity** emerged as the public-facing OER layer through a deliberate decision process. The choice was between Brightspace (institutionally familiar but walled) and Wikiversity (genuinely open). The resolution: "always public" with "lack of publicity through obscurity." Materials exist openly from inception on Wikiversity but aren't actively promoted until ready. This enables transparency without premature exposure while making the "open" in OER authentic rather than aspirational.
### The "Always Public" Decision
This deserves elaboration because it reflects a distinctive operational philosophy. Rather than developing in private and publishing when "ready," the AIX OER project operates in public from day one. The practical logic:
- No authentication barrier—any faculty member anywhere can use materials immediately
- Discoverable via search engines, citeable with stable URLs
- Public revision history documents how AI-assisted curriculum development actually works
- Models the archive-as-research-data approach central to the methodology
- Obscurity provides de facto access control during development; active promotion happens later
This approach was tested through the Wikiversity deployment, where the initial site structure (AIX_OER root page with subpages for courses, modules, participants, and process documentation) was built publicly before participant onboarding was complete.
### Weekly Living with AI Sessions
Monday evening sessions (7–9 PM) serve multiple functions simultaneously:
- **Showcase space**: Participants present works-in-progress
- **Collective learning**: Open discussions about daily AI use
- **Documentation creation**: Sessions are recorded and edited into podcast episodes
- **Community maintenance**: Regular synchronous contact sustains the collaborative
Chris Smith hosts the Living with AI podcast, with AIX OER participants and AI Fellows providing the guest roster. Approximately 25 episodes recorded as of February 2026. The podcast is not supplementary content—it's integral infrastructure, creating durable artifacts from ephemeral conversations.
### Participant Recruitment and Onboarding
The invitation process ran through the AI Fellows network and SUNY Poly community. The timeline:
- **Dec 17–22, 2025**: Platform setup, initial ideation among first stakeholders
- **Dec 28–Jan 10, 2026**: Synthesis, refinement, onboarding new participants
- **Jan 11–19**: Pre-launch revision, informal meetups (online + possible in-person)
- **Jan 20**: Soft launch (hybrid: online + in-person options)
- **Jan 27–May 7**: Weekly Monday sessions + ongoing collaborative work
Co-authorship agreements were drafted addressing human subjects considerations while enabling collaborative knowledge production. The intent: all participants as co-authors of resulting OER materials, pending ethics review.

## II. COURSE IMPLEMENTATIONS: WHAT'S RUNNING AND WHAT'S DESIGNED
### IDT 590: Designing Critical AI Literacy Curricula (Spring 2026—Active)
The graduate seminar launched Spring 2026 as the academic vehicle for AIX OER work. Central features:
**Reflexive practice as methodology**: Participants maintain archives of their own AI-supported reading, thinking, and writing. These archives serve as primary source material—not supplementary documentation—for understanding how AI literacy is learned and taught.
**Community of practice model**: Graduate students, faculty, librarians, and instructional designers work together in Hyflex format (in-person, synchronous online, asynchronous). The course explicitly brings together people from different institutional roles rather than segregating by status.
**The course IS the project**: IDT 590 participants are AIX OER collaborators. Students learn AI literacy by practicing the methodology the development team creates. The process IS the pedagogy—a reflexive loop where the thing being studied is also the thing being done.
**Definition embedded in practice**: "AI literacy is the ability to read, think, and write using the tools, techniques, and systems of artificial intelligence in dialogue with one's peers." The "in dialogue with one's peers" clause is operationalized through the collaborative structure itself.
### AI-101: 2-Credit Skills and Ethics Foundation (Developed and Working)
AI-101 exists as a standalone 2-credit course providing baseline AI fluency combined with ethical grounding. It synthesizes three typically parallel approaches:
1. **Technical foundations**: via Google AI Essentials and Prompting Essentials certificates
2. **Humanistic inquiry**: via contemporary scholarship on AI ethics, labor, and meaning
3. **Material grounding**: via the Munich "City in the Cloud, Data on the Ground" exhibit concept
The course design interrogates what the Google certificates don't address. Students complete certificates for operational fluency, then the course asks: Who builds AI systems? Under what conditions? For whose benefit? What are the planetary costs? This creates the fluency/literacy distinction in practice—certificates develop fluency, the course develops literacy.
AI-101 functions as the foundation layer in the broader curricular architecture, pairing with disciplinary X88 courses to create comprehensive AI literacy pathways.
### AI-Intensive First Year Experience (FYAI) Programs
The FYAI proposal creates structured first-year pathways integrating AI literacy with Gen Ed requirements. Two primary variants emerged through iterative design:
**FYAI-3** (flexible first-semester requirements): Students take three disciplinary 188-level courses (e.g., POS 188, ART 188, COM 188) plus AI-101 and 1–2 major courses. Total: 15–17 credits. Satisfies multiple Gen Ed requirements while delivering comprehensive AI literacy.
**FYAI-2** (STEM students with mandatory Math/Science): Students take two 188-level courses plus required Math and Natural Science courses. Total: 15–16 credits. Identified as the optimal solution for STEM majors—integrates AI literacy while accommodating non-negotiable first-semester requirements.
The FYAI design reflects a key operational insight: AI literacy programs must build on infrastructure already in place rather than requiring new standalone courses. Students develop AI literacy while completing actual degree requirements, not in addition to them.
### X88 Framework: Discipline-Specific AI Literacy Courses
The X88 concept (188/288/388/488/588) provides a shared numeric identifier for AI literacy courses across disciplines and levels. Each course explicitly addresses the Information Literacy competency with artificial intelligence as focal technology, at the sophistication level appropriate to its students.
Potential examples:
- **CS 188**: First-year computational contexts
- **HIS 388**: Junior-level historical research with AI
- **BUS 588**: Graduate seminar on AI in organizational decision-making
SUNY Poly's catalog conveniently contains only one existing "88" course, making the identifier available. The X88 framework may serve as a transitional component (2–4 years) as the institution determines its long-term AI literacy integration strategy.
The flexible curriculum concept underlying X88 enables one document framework to generate infinite disciplinary variants, structured along configurable dimensions: AI definition (narrow vs. broad), discipline, technical complexity, pedagogical approach, and course level.
### "AI and the Human Future" Course Design
A standalone, intro-level STS (Science and Technology Studies) social science course designed for 24–48 students. Distinguishing features:
**Three-stage progression**: Manual validation (small corpus, ~36 sources) → Machine validation (medium corpus, 50–100 sources) → Informed stewardship (large corpus, 100+ sources). Students learn to work at AI-enabled scale while maintaining intellectual responsibility.
**Workbench methodology**: Students maintain personal LLM workspaces with three-folder architecture (Transcripts/Synthesis Docs/Outputs). Faculty model each assignment by presenting interrogated primary sources in lectures; students replicate with the same corpus.
**Weekly pattern**: Question of the Day → individual AI response → group discussion → continued individual thread → sharing complete transcripts.
**Pedagogical architecture**: The complicated challenge is verification. How do you teach students to trust (and not trust) AI analysis at scale? The course addresses this through progressive scaling—starting with manageable corpora where manual checking is feasible, building toward larger collections where informed judgment replaces exhaustive verification.
### DesignWriteStudio / IDT 575/COM 375: Designing and Writing Interactive Texts
A hypertext literacy course using a three-strand architecture with different AI constraints:
- **Wikipedia (Red—No AI)**: Human-only editing, learning traditional knowledge commons infrastructure
- **TiddlyWiki (Yellow—Annotation)**: AI-assisted analysis and documentation
- **Wikiversity (Green—Full AI Collaboration)**: AI as compositional partner with mandatory documentation
The "Traffic Light" framework creates pedagogical friction by requiring students to work simultaneously across platforms with different rules. This isn't accidental constraint—it's designed to make the differences between unassisted, partially assisted, and fully AI-collaborative work experientially visible.
Key operational findings from early implementation:
- Students defaulted to asking "what is hypertext?" rather than following the assigned introspective prompt, suggesting too many concepts introduced simultaneously (Ted Nelson, three platforms, Traffic Light system, MediaWiki syntax)
- Asynchronous students must be treated as "first-class citizens" with full access to materials, not positioned as having "missed" synchronous sessions
- Technology setup consumed more time than anticipated; distributing tool setup across multiple sessions prevents overwhelm

## III. FACULTY DEVELOPMENT: FROM THEORY TO WORKSHOP
### SUNY Potsdam Faculty AI Days (January 13–16, 2026)
Co-presented with Michelle Malinovsky, these workshops represented the first major test of translating the RTW framework into faculty development practice.
**Workshop structure**: Two sessions (approximately 2.5 hours) with a work session in the middle. Faculty engage with AI systems directly, then facilitators analyze the collective output during the break and present findings in the second session.
**The four-prompt sequence**: Faculty follow a structured dialogue progression:
1. **Prompt 1 (Thinking)**: Engage in dialogue about thinking itself—"What happens in your brain before words appear?"—with 5 questions each direction between human and AI
2. **Prompt 2 (Reading)**: Upload three readings (Searle's "Chinese Room," Bender et al.'s "Stochastic Parrots," Bubeck et al.'s "Sparks of AGI") and produce progressive synthesis
3. **Prompt 3 (Writing)**: Generate an encyclopedic entry synthesizing the dialogue and readings
4. **Prompt 4 (Export Preparation)**: Structure the transcript with metadata, participant identifier, and CC-BY license information
**Facilitator analysis workflow**: During the break, facilitators collect exported transcripts, concatenate them, upload to an LLM, and run analysis prompts to identify patterns across the group. This enables collective discussion about what the group's interactions reveal about AI literacy practice—modeling the MICA methodology at workshop scale.
**Division of labor**: Michelle leads Session 1 introduction, toolkit demo, and framework presentation. Steve circulates during the exercise, handles technical processing during the break, presents analysis findings, and leads the archive-as-OER discussion.
**"AI as Tool, Not Cheat" reframing**: The workshops actively counter the dominant faculty anxiety (students cheating with AI) by positioning AI as a literacy tool that requires development of genuine cognitive engagement. The four-prompt sequence demonstrates this—you can't complete it without actually thinking.
### Workshop Adaptation Principles
The Potsdam experience generated transferable insights:
- **Shorter format (90 min)**: Cut to 2 prompts (Prompt 1 + Prompt 3), eliminate break analysis, focus on process
- **Longer format (half day)**: Add breakout sessions for discipline-specific prompt design
- **Discipline-specific**: Replace readings with texts from specific field
- **Asynchronous**: Participants complete exercise before workshop; session focuses entirely on analysis and discussion
### RTW Framework Applied to Workshop Activities
The workshop structure explicitly maps to the literacy framework:
- **Reading**: Faculty ingest and interpret three scholarly positions on AI and thinking
- **Thinking**: Faculty engage in reflexive dialogue about their own cognitive processes, then evaluate AI-generated synthesis
- **Writing**: Faculty produce compositional output (encyclopedic entry) through AI collaboration
This isn't accidental alignment—it's deliberate pedagogy. Faculty experience the RTW framework by practicing it, not by hearing about it.

## IV. THEORETICAL DEVELOPMENTS: WHAT'S NEW
### The Three-Pillar vs. Four-Pillar Resolution
A significant definitional question emerged and was resolved: Should AI literacy be defined as RTW (Reading, Thinking, Writing) or RTWL (adding "Living")?
**Resolution**: RTW as the literacy definition; "Living with AI" as program framing.
The reasoning: RTW is the transhistorical, descriptive definition of literacy that applies across technological periods. "Living with AI" names the experiential and developmental dimension—the fact that AI literacy is practiced daily, not just in classrooms. But "Living" is not a cognitive operation parallel to reading, thinking, and writing. It's the environment in which those operations occur.
This distinction matters operationally: the Living with AI podcast frames the ongoing practice; the RTW framework defines what literacy IS.
### "Consistent with One's Peers" Clause
The full literacy definition includes a crucial qualifier: "reading, thinking, and writing using the tools, techniques, and technological systems *consistent with one's peers*."
This clause does significant work:
- **Disciplinary variation**: "Peers" means different things in different contexts—engineers solving problems, historians interpreting sources, nurses coordinating care. Literacy is always situated.
- **Developmental appropriateness**: First-year students have different peers than graduate students. The clause enables progressive sophistication without changing the definition.
- **Gatekeeping function**: Those whose practices don't align with peer expectations face structural exclusion—not through malice but through misalignment. This is descriptive, not prescriptive.
- **Temporal sensitivity**: As professional contexts change, "consistent with peers" shifts automatically. The definition tracks practice rather than prescribing it.
Cross-document analysis revealed this clause was occasionally dropping from implementations—a form of definition drift that weakened the framework's disciplinary portability.
### "AI Literacy" vs. "Generative AI Literacy"
A strategic language decision resolved across contexts:
**"AI Literacy"** serves as the umbrella term—appropriate for SUNY Gen Ed alignment, grant rhetoric, public communication, and theoretical integrity. It's future-proof and doesn't limit the framework to current technology.
**"Generative AI Literacy"** specifies current methods when pedagogical precision requires it. Used when describing what students actually do with current tools (prompting LLMs, evaluating generated text) rather than the broader competency being developed.
The operational pattern: use "AI Literacy" in framing documents, policy language, and course titles. Use "Generative AI" when describing specific activities, assignments, and tool interactions.
### The Communication + Information Literacy Combination
A key theoretical development since the first synthesis: the recognition that SUNY's Communication requirement and Information Literacy competency, taken together, already define AI literacy without using that term.
**Communication** describes the cognitive operations: reading (research, organize), thinking (develop argument, evaluate), writing (demonstrate communication, revise).
**Information Literacy** describes the material context: tools (appropriate to need and discipline), techniques (evaluate authority, validity, bias, origin), technological systems (traditional sources OR emerging technologies like AI).
Combined: **literacy is reading, thinking, and writing using the tools, techniques, and technological systems appropriate to one's discipline**—which IS the project's definition. This structural insight means AI literacy integration doesn't require new framework architecture. It requires making visible what the existing framework already contains.
### Information Stewardship Model
Students positioned not as "AI users" but as information stewards who verify sources, evaluate AI output, and take responsibility for information accuracy. This reframes the relationship:
- **Not**: Student uses AI tool to produce output
- **But**: Student maintains intellectual ownership through evaluation and revision decisions while AI handles drafting
The stewardship model connects to assessment practices—documentation requirements (conversation archives, process portfolios) make stewardship visible and assessable.

## V. ASSESSMENT: HOW TO KNOW IT'S WORKING
### Against Detection/Prohibition
The operational stance crystallized: "Don't police! Just teach!" Detection approaches (Turnitin AI detection, etc.) undermine integrity cultivation. The goal isn't catching AI use—it's requiring documentation of it.
Practical mechanism: Export your conversation. Show your evaluation process. Demonstrate verification of sources. This forces conscious engagement because you can't document a process you didn't actively manage.
### Assessment Through Documentation
Three primary mechanisms:
**Process portfolios**: Students collect evidence of their AI-mediated reading, thinking, and writing over time. The portfolio demonstrates development, not just final products.
**Hybrid assessments with oral defense**: Written work (potentially AI-assisted) combined with oral examination where students must demonstrate understanding of what they produced. The oral component reveals whether literacy (cognitive engagement) occurred, not just fluency (technical operation).
**Annotated submissions**: Students submit work with conversation archives showing how AI was used. The annotation reveals the thinking—where did the student redirect the AI? What did they reject? What did they verify? These decisions ARE the literacy.
### The Neural Activation Criterion
A practical test for whether AI literacy is occurring: genuine cognitive engagement must happen. A student who copies AI explanations without evaluating accuracy demonstrates fluency but not literacy. The flashcard example from the first synthesis remains the touchstone: generating flashcards shows fluency; deciding whether to trust them despite a 20% error rate requires literacy.
Documentation requirements serve as literacy assessment because they force the neural activation that distinguishes literacy from fluency. You can't write meaningful annotations of a process you didn't think about.

## VI. CHALLENGES AND ADAPTATIONS
### Definition Drift
**Problem**: Theoretical clarity degraded as concepts moved from foundational documents to implementation materials. The "consistent with one's peers" clause disappeared. "Living with AI" expanded from program name to fourth pillar. "AI Literacy" and "Generative AI Literacy" were used interchangeably.
**Solution**: Establishing the first Opus synthesis and the SUNY Gen Ed essay as canonical touchstones. When documents diverge, these are the reference points. Systematic consistency checking across the document set revealed drift patterns that could be corrected before publication.
### Scope Management
**Problem**: The project encompasses course development, faculty workshops, policy analysis, OER creation, podcast production, international partnerships, and system-wide implementation support. Scope creep is constant.
**Solution**: The four-initiative structure (AIX OER collaborative, IDT 590 seminar, X88 flexible curriculum, Living with AI podcast) provides organizational containers. Each initiative has distinct deliverables while sharing the theoretical framework. When new opportunities arise, they're assessed against these containers rather than spawning new initiatives.
### The DesignWriteStudio Complexity Problem
**Problem**: The IDT 575/COM 375 course introduced too many concepts simultaneously—Ted Nelson, three platforms, Traffic Light system, MediaWiki syntax, AI collaboration, hypertext theory. Students defaulted to simpler questions rather than engaging the assigned introspective prompt.
**Adaptation**: Progressive disclosure became the operative principle. Distribute tool setup across multiple sessions. Front-load intellectual engagement over administrative tasks. Establish reflexive AI documentation practices from day one. Create immediate student production rather than extended preparation.
**Broader lesson**: AI literacy pedagogy must manage cognitive load carefully. The instinct to teach everything about AI at once—tools, ethics, infrastructure, critical thinking—overwhelms. Start with practice, introduce theory as it becomes necessary for practice.
### Platform Friction
**Problem**: Wikiversity's DynamicPageList uses the legacy "Intersection" version with different syntax than documented in most tutorials. MediaWiki's 1990s infrastructure creates genuine usability challenges for students accustomed to modern web interfaces.
**Adaptation**: Frame platform limitations as pedagogical opportunities. MediaWiki's "clunkiness" teaches infrastructure literacy—understanding how real-world knowledge systems work, not just how polished apps present information. Provide clear navigational guides while acknowledging the learning curve.
### Asynchronous Equity
**Problem**: Hyflex courses risk making asynchronous students second-class participants who "missed" the live experience.
**Solution**: Treat asynchronous access as a distinct mode of engagement, not a deficit. Synthesis blog posts on Wikiversity provide different—not lesser—access to course content. Position different engagement modes as equally valuable paths to the same learning outcomes.

## VIII. TOOLS AND INFRASTRUCTURE
### The Archive Principle (Operational)
"Without those exports, the thinking evaporates. With them, it compounds."
This principle drives daily practice:
1. Daily AI practice documented through conversation
2. Markdown exports saved to Teams folders (SharePoint-backed)
3. Exports tagged with metadata (timestamp, model, topic)
4. Weekly synthesis and cross-cutting analysis
5. Pattern identification feeds into collaborative development
The "/ark" command triggers systematic conversation archiving with metadata headers (title, summary, participants, date, key concepts). This is not administrative overhead—it's the research methodology in action. Every archived conversation becomes potential primary source material for collective analysis.
### WisdomMaker Workflow
The umbrella term for the archiving/knowledge management pipeline:
- **One-click conversation export** from multiple AI platforms (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini)
- **"Tiddlerization"**: Atomizing content for TiddlyWiki integration
- **Cross-model fertilization**: Moving insights between different AI systems
- **Verification and validation**: Checking AI outputs against sources
- **Knowledge base construction**: Building cumulative understanding from conversation archives
The goal is multi-model infrastructure for collaborative knowledge production that transforms raw dialogue into durable knowledge through systematic documentation.

## IX. WHAT'S WORKING, WHAT'S CHALLENGING, WHAT'S UNRESOLVED
### Working
- **The RTW framework provides genuine disciplinary portability**: Biology, sociology, history, arts—the definition adapts through the "tools, techniques, and systems appropriate to the discipline" clause without requiring modification
- **Documentation requirements as assessment**: Making thinking visible through conversation archives provides richer evidence of learning than traditional submissions
- **The four-prompt workshop structure**: Faculty engage immediately with AI practice rather than debating whether AI should be allowed—the workshop bypasses resistance by creating the experience
- **Always-public development**: Wikiversity hosting enables genuine openness while obscurity provides practical access control during development
- **Reflexive methodology**: Using AI to build courses about AI creates productive loops where the process generates both course materials and research data
### Challenging
- **Cognitive load in multi-platform courses**: Students (and faculty) can be overwhelmed by simultaneous introduction of new tools, concepts, and platforms
- **Definition consistency across documents**: As materials multiply, maintaining theoretical coherence requires active maintenance—touchstone documents and systematic checking
- **Scale**: The $1,500 budget supports 10 Claude Team participants for 5 months. Scaling to 64 campuses requires fundamentally different resource models
- **Faculty resistance**: Some faculty remain in "AI as cheating" frameworks that prevent engagement with literacy development approaches
- **Assessment validity**: Documentation requirements assess process but rely on honest documentation. Students who fabricate or selectively edit archives undermine the assessment model
### Unresolved
- **Long-term institutional strategy**: X88 courses may be transitional (2–4 years). What replaces them? Full integration across all courses? Permanent standalone offerings? The answer depends on how rapidly AI becomes genuinely infrastructural
- **International scaling**: Dr. Adebayo's context (100,000 online students, resource-constrained environment) surfaces assumptions about access, bandwidth, and tool availability that developed-world frameworks take for granted
- **The verification problem**: As students work with larger corpora, manual verification becomes impossible. "Informed stewardship" is the conceptual answer, but operational mechanisms for teaching it at scale are still developing
- **Podcast as pedagogy**: Living with AI episodes are being recorded, but systematic integration as course material (students engaging episodes as texts, producing content for the podcast) hasn't been fully operationalized
- **Sustainability beyond the fellowship**: The AI Fellows cohort provides institutional infrastructure through 2025–2026. What sustains the work after?
